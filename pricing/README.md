# PricePilot Pricing

**Demand estimation that refuses to answer more often than it answers.**

This package turns order history into a price proposal. It is the probabilistic half of PricePilot; the [enforcer](../enforcer) is the half that must never be wrong.

## The one idea that matters

**Elasticity is identified by variation in price, not by volume of sales.**

A SKU with 300,000 units sold at a single price contains exactly zero information about what happens at a different price. An estimator that returns a number anyway is worse than useless, because that number looks identical to a real one. `TestVolumeWithoutPriceVariationIdentifiesNothing` makes this executable: 303,620 units over a year, and the estimator refuses.

Refusal is a first-class outcome here, not an error path. On a real catalog most SKUs land there, and saying so plainly is the product working.

| Refusal | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `too_few_days` | not enough history to fit |
| `volume_too_low` | fewer than 45 units a day on average; the estimator is not validated there (see below) |
| `too_few_distinct_prices` | never repriced enough to identify a slope |
| `insufficient_price_variation` | repriced, but not enough to separate from noise |
| `not_distinguishable_from_zero` | the interval contains 0 |
| `implausible_positive_elasticity` | demand rising with price means something else drives both |
| `interval_too_wide` | an estimate exists but is too imprecise to price against |

## What is measured, not asserted

Because the data is synthetic, the true elasticity is known — which is the entire reason for generating it. On real merchant data you can measure that an estimate is *stable*; you can never measure that it is *right*.

```
true -1.40 -> -1.477  err 5.5%   R2=0.663
true -1.80 -> -1.679  err 6.7%   R2=0.720
true -2.50 -> -2.550  err 2.0%   R2=0.793
true -3.20 -> -3.354  err 4.8%   R2=0.830

95% interval covered the truth in 569/600 trials (94.8%)
40 sparse SKUs: MSE 0.2020 standalone -> 0.0894 pooled (56% reduction)
```

Every figure above is measured at about 45 units a day per SKU, with the volume gate switched off in those tests so their data and seeds stay exactly as published.

The coverage number is the one to read. A stated 95% interval that contains the truth 94.8% of the time is an interval doing its job; one that is merely printed and never checked is decoration.

Counts are Poisson draws around a lognormally-shocked mean, so the data is mildly **misspecified** relative to the log-log model being fitted. That is deliberate — order counts really are discrete and overdispersed, and an estimator that only recovers truth from data generated by its own assumptions has not been tested.

## Where it stops working

Taking the log of a small count is biased, and the intervals stay narrow around the wrong value, so nothing downstream notices. `cmd/sweep` measures it: 10,000 trials for each of eleven demand levels and three true elasticities, seeds fixed before the run, and a pass rule written down before the results were seen (for every elasticity, the lower 95% bound of coverage is at least 92% and bias is at most 3%). Levels at 40 units a day and above pass. Twenty fails: at a true elasticity of −3, coverage is 90.9% and bias 3.1%. By 3 units a day coverage is 62%, and by 2 it is 8%.

The shipped floor is 45 realised units a day, the smallest volume in the lowest passing level, rounded up. Below it the estimator returns `volume_too_low`. The full output is in [`testdata/volume_sweep.txt`](testdata/volume_sweep.txt).

Two things this does not fix. One level (5 a day) passes the rule in isolation, because coverage is measured only among runs the other refusals let through; the floor ignores it, since every level above it up to 40 fails. And callers must pass days with no sales as observations with zero units, or a slow product looks busier than it is.

## The confounder, priced in dollars

Promotions cut price and lift demand simultaneously. An estimator that ignores them credits the entire promotional lift to the price cut:

```
true elasticity     -2.00
with promo control  -2.089
without             -3.151
```

That is not an academic point about a coefficient. Run it through the optimal-price formula on a $10.00 unit cost:

```
true optimum        $20.00
with promo control  $19.18
without             $14.65   ← underprices by 26.8%
```

One omitted control, 27% of the price. This is the same shape of error as training on a feature that leaks the outcome — the model is not broken, it is answering a different question than the one asked.

## Pooling, scoped correctly

Sparse SKUs borrow strength from their neighbours by DerSimonian-Laird random-effects shrinkage: a SKU with a tight interval barely moves, one with a wide interval is pulled most of the way.

Two decisions worth defending:

**Pooling is within a collection, never across the catalog.** An early version pooled all SKUs together and dragged a well-identified SKU with a year of clean data from 3% error to 15%, because it was being shrunk toward a mean that included wholesale bags and a mug. Shrinking toward products that do not share a demand curve does not reduce error, it imports someone else's.

**Wrong-signed estimates still contribute to the mean.** Dropping them first would be selecting on the outcome — discarding one tail of the sampling distribution and biasing the pooled mean toward the answer we were hoping for. A confounded SKU normally carries a large standard error and is down-weighted on its own merits.

An estimate is only reported as `pooled` when shrinkage actually moved it. A SKU pulled 2% is still standing on its own data, and labelling it otherwise would misreport where the number came from.

## Where it stops

`Propose` refuses in three places the estimator alone cannot catch. The third is the one worth reading twice:

- no unit cost, so there is no margin to optimise
- the point estimate implies no interior optimum (β ≥ −1)
- **the interval reaches β = −1 even though the point estimate does not.** The optimal-price formula p\* = c·β/(1+β) has a discontinuity there. A point estimate of −1.25 looks comfortably elastic, but if its interval reaches −0.93 the data is consistent with a world where no optimal price exists at all. Pricing off the midpoint of an interval that straddles a discontinuity is how a confident number becomes a wrong one.

## Known limitations

- **Synthetic data only.** Nothing here has touched a real merchant's orders. The estimator is proven to recover a known β; it is not proven to work on real demand, which is non-stationary, cross-elastic, and full of stockouts.
- **No cross-price effects.** Every SKU is estimated independently, so cannibalisation between a 250g and a 1kg bag is invisible.
- **Zero-unit days are dropped**, because they cannot be logged. This biases toward SKUs that sell steadily.
- **Constant elasticity is an assumption**, not a finding. Real demand curves bend.
- **No revenue-lift claim is made anywhere**, because proving lift requires a live A/B on a real store.
